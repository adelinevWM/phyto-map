What this map is for

Phytomining grows plants that draw metals out of the ground, then harvests the plants and recovers the metal from them. The idea examined here is to try it on abandoned coal mine land in Pennsylvania, where the ground is already disturbed and where rare earth elements have turned up in coal waste and in the sludge left behind when acid mine drainage is treated.

Before anyone can judge whether that is realistic, there has to be an answer to a plain question: where have rare earth elements in coal mine waste actually been measured, and how concentrated were they? This map collects every published measurement we have found so far and places it against the state's inventory of abandoned mine land.

How to read it

The round markers are sites where coal waste or mine drainage solids were sampled and analysed. Darker color means a higher concentration of rare earth elements plus yttrium. Click a marker to see every sample from that site and where the numbers come from. The green shapes are abandoned mine land problem areas recorded by the Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection. The small gray dots are a national survey of ordinary soil, included only so the mine waste numbers have something to be compared against; there are few of them in Pennsylvania and none of them are mine sites.

Concentrations are given in milligrams per kilogram, which is the same as parts per million. "REE+Y" means the fourteen lanthanide elements, lanthanum through lutetium, plus yttrium, which behaves like them and is usually counted with them. Scandium is not included. The 300 ppm line in the legend is the feed grade the U.S. Department of Energy's rare earth program uses for coal-based resources in its pilot-plant goals. It is a benchmark for extracting the elements directly from coal materials, not a threshold for phytomining.

What it does not show

It does not show how much of the metal a plant would take up. That depends on soil acidity, on the fraction of the metal that is actually available to roots, and on the plant species, none of which a total concentration tells you. It does not show who owns a site, whether it can be reached, or whether it is being reclaimed. It covers only measurements that have been published with coordinates; results held in agency or company files are not here. The mine land and coal waste layers cover Pennsylvania only, while the soil survey covers the whole country. And the number of sites is small. That is not a flaw in the map; it is the finding. The measurements needed to assess phytomining on Pennsylvania mine land mostly have not been made.

How the numbers were derived

For the McDevitt samples, REE+Y is the dataset's own Total_REE column for each site's "total" row, which sums the sequential leach steps; yttrium is the Y value from the same row, converted from micrograms to milligrams per kilogram. For the two Levitan releases, REE+Y is the sum of La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, Lu, and Y from Table 3 on the whole-sample basis ("ws"); for the anthracite release the RAW rows are used, not the SINK fractions. Clean coal and raw coal samples in the bituminous release are omitted because they are not waste. Values below detection are treated as zero. Every number can be checked against the linked releases.
